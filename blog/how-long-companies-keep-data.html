Why there's no single legal number

People often ask "how long can a company legally keep my data" expecting a single number, but neither GDPR nor CCPA sets one universal limit. Instead, both laws require that data only be kept as long as it's actually needed for the purpose it was collected for — a principle GDPR calls "storage limitation." The actual number of months or years depends entirely on what the data is and why it was collected.

The rule that actually governs this: storage limitation

Storage limitation means a company can't just hold your data indefinitely "in case it's useful later." If you ask why they still have data from an account you closed two years ago, they're required to have a specific, current justification — not just "we keep everything." This is one of the more useful angles for a deletion request: instead of just asking them to delete your data, ask them to justify why they're still retaining it under their own stated retention policy.

What about backups and archived copies?

This is where most companies have legitimate wiggle room. Even after a deletion request is processed in the live system, backups are often on a separate schedule and can take longer to purge — commonly 30 to 90 days, sometimes longer for infrequently-rotated backup systems. This isn't a loophole exactly; regulators generally accept that immediate deletion from every backup snapshot isn't technically realistic, as long as the company discloses the backup retention timeline and the data isn't actively used from those backups in the meantime.

Industry-specific retention rules

Beyond the general storage limitation principle, several industries have their own specific minimums layered on top: financial services (five to ten years, covered in our banking data guide), healthcare (six to ten years or longer, covered in our HIPAA vs GDPR guide), and telecommunications (often six months to two years for call and connection records, depending on the country's specific data retention laws). When a company cites "industry regulation" as a reason for retention, it's worth asking exactly which regulation and what the specific retention period is — a vague answer is a sign the excuse hasn't actually been checked internally.

How to find out a specific company's policy

Most companies publish their retention periods in their privacy policy, usually under a section literally called "How long we keep your data" or similar. If it's vague or missing, that's itself worth raising in a formal request — a company that can't state its retention period likely isn't enforcing one consistently.

The loophole: anonymized and aggregated data

Storage limitation only applies to personal data — information that can identify you. Once a company genuinely anonymizes data (not just removes your name, but strips it of anything that could be used to re-identify you, even in combination with other datasets), it falls outside GDPR's scope entirely and can be kept indefinitely. This is a legitimate distinction, but it's also one companies sometimes stretch — "anonymized" data that's actually just pseudonymized (your name replaced with an ID number that still maps back to you internally) is still personal data and still subject to retention limits. If a company claims your data is "anonymized" as a reason they can't delete it, it's fair to ask them to confirm whether it's truly anonymized or merely pseudonymized.

Why "we deleted it" doesn't always mean it's gone from every system

Even a fully compliant deletion typically doesn't purge backup systems instantly — most privacy policies disclose a separate, usually longer window (30 to 90 days is common) for data to clear from backups and disaster-recovery systems after the "live" deletion is confirmed. This isn't a violation; it's standard infrastructure practice, and reputable companies state it explicitly. If a company's policy doesn't mention backup retention at all, that's a gap worth asking about directly rather than assuming it doesn't apply to them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a maximum retention period under GDPR?

GDPR doesn't set one fixed number — it requires retention to be limited to what's necessary for the stated purpose, which varies by data type and industry.

Can I ask a company how long they keep my data?

Yes, this falls under your right of access — you can request their retention period for your specific data as part of an access request.

Do cookies and analytics data follow the same rules?

Yes, though in practice cookie retention is often shorter (commonly 12-26 months) and should be disclosed in the company's cookie policy.

Why does data sometimes linger in backups after deletion?

Backup systems are usually on a separate rotation schedule from live systems, so a short delay (often 30-90 days) before backups are fully purged is generally considered acceptable if disclosed.

Is pseudonymized data the same as anonymized data?

No — pseudonymized data can still be linked back to you through a key the company holds, and remains subject to GDPR, while truly anonymized data cannot be re-identified and falls outside the law's scope.

Why does deleted data sometimes still show up after a request is confirmed?

It's usually still clearing from backup or disaster-recovery systems, which most companies disclose as a separate, slightly longer retention window than the main deletion.
