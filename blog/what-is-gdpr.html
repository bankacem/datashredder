What is GDPR? Complete Guide to Your Rights & the Right to Erasure

Updated: July 31, 2026 · 9 min read

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the European Union's data protection law, in force since May 2018. It doesn't just apply to companies based in Europe — any organization anywhere in the world that offers goods or services to people in the EU/EEA, or monitors their behavior, must follow it. That means most large platforms you use every day — Google, Meta, Amazon, TikTok, LinkedIn — are bound by it, no matter where you live.

This guide explains, in plain English, what rights GDPR actually gives you, how the "right to be forgotten" works, and the exact steps to use it.

The 8 Rights GDPR Gives You

GDPR treats you as a "data subject" with specific, enforceable rights over your own personal data:

  • Right to be informed — companies must clearly explain what data they collect and why.
  • Right of access — you can request a full copy of the data a company holds about you.
  • Right to rectification — you can have inaccurate or incomplete data corrected.
  • Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten") — you can request deletion of your data under Article 17.
  • Right to restrict processing — you can limit how your data is used without deleting it.
  • Right to data portability — you can request your data in a machine-readable format to move it elsewhere.
  • Right to object — you can object to processing, including for direct marketing.
  • Rights related to automated decision-making — you can challenge decisions made solely by algorithms, including profiling.

This article focuses on the right most people actually want to use: erasure.

The 6 Legal Grounds for Erasure (Article 17)

You can request deletion when at least one of these applies:

  1. The data is no longer necessary for the purpose it was originally collected for.
  2. You withdraw consent, and there is no other legal basis for processing.
  3. You object to the processing, and the company has no overriding legitimate grounds to continue.
  4. The data has been processed unlawfully.
  5. Erasure is required to comply with a legal obligation.
  6. The data was collected in relation to offering services to a child.

When a Company Can Refuse (The Exceptions)

The right to erasure is powerful, but it's not absolute. A company can lawfully refuse, in whole or in part, when the data is needed for:

  • Exercising the right of freedom of expression and information.
  • Compliance with a legal obligation (e.g. tax or accounting records).
  • Reasons of public interest, such as public health.
  • Archiving, scientific/historical research, or statistical purposes.
  • The establishment, exercise, or defense of legal claims (e.g. an active dispute or fraud investigation).

If a company refuses, it must give you a specific, written legal reason — not a vague policy statement.

How Long Does a Company Have to Respond?

Under Article 12(3), a company must respond within one calendar month of receiving your request. This can be extended by two further months for complex requests, but the company must tell you within the first month if it's doing that, and explain why.

How to Actually Submit an Erasure Request

  1. Identify the right recipient. Look for a "Privacy," "Data Protection Officer (DPO)," or "Legal" email address, usually in a company's privacy policy.
  2. State your identity clearly so the company can verify who you are without you having to send more data than necessary.
  3. Cite Article 17 explicitly and state which of the six grounds applies to you.
  4. Ask for confirmation in writing once deletion is complete, and ask them to notify any third parties they shared your data with.
  5. Keep a copy of your request and the date you sent it — you'll need this if you have to escalate later.

What If the Company Ignores or Refuses You?

If a month passes with no response, or you get a refusal that doesn't cite a valid legal reason, you have two free options:

  • File a complaint with your national Data Protection Authority (DPA) — this is free, and every EU/EEA country has one (e.g. the CNIL in France, the ICO in the UK, the AEPD in Spain).
  • Escalate directly with the company, referencing your original request date and asking them to confirm compliance or provide the specific legal basis for refusal in writing.

Companies cannot charge you a fee for a normal erasure request. The only exception is if your requests are "manifestly unfounded or excessive" — for example, sending the same request dozens of times.

GDPR vs. Similar Laws Elsewhere
