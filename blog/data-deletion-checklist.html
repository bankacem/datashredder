Before you send anything

The most common mistake in data deletion isn't the request itself — it's skipping the prep. Deletion is permanent, and once it's processed there's no undo. Before you request anything, download or export whatever you might want to keep: photos, order history, saved messages. Most platforms have a data export tool built for exactly this.

The full checklist

  1. List where your data actually lives. Search your own name, check old email accounts for signup confirmations, and think through every social, shopping, and app account you've created.
  2. Back up anything you want to keep using each platform's export tool, before requesting anything.
  3. Try the platform's built-in deletion option first. It's usually faster than a formal request when one exists — see our platform-by-platform guide.
  4. Send a formal erasure request for anything without a self-serve option, citing the correct law for your situation.
  5. Note the date you sent it, and the legal deadline that applies (30–45 days depending on the law).
  6. Follow up once, in writing, if the deadline passes — don't send repeated emails hoping for a different outcome.
  7. Escalate to a regulator if there's still no valid response — see our GDPR complaint guide.
  8. Check the data broker sites that pull from public records, since these operate independently of the original company — our data broker guide covers the priority list.
  9. Recheck in 60–90 days. Confirm the data is actually gone by searching for yourself again, especially on people-search sites, which can re-populate.

After you've sent your request

Keep a simple log — company name, date sent, deadline, and outcome. It sounds like overkill for one request, but if you're working through even five or six companies, it's the only way to know who's overdue without re-checking your inbox constantly.

Mistakes that slow the whole process down

  • Sending a vague request. "Please delete my data" without specifying what data or citing a legal basis often gets a slower, less complete response than a scoped, specific one.
  • Forgetting to back up first. Once deletion is confirmed, there's no retrieving photos or order history afterward.
  • Escalating too early or too late. Jumping to a regulator complaint before the legal deadline has even passed wastes the complaint's weight; waiting six months after an ignored deadline loses momentum on the other end.
  • Not checking data brokers separately. Deleting your account with the original company doesn't touch broker copies built from public records or previous data sales.

Keeping your footprint small going forward

Deletion is a cleanup, not a permanent fix — new accounts and new data collection start the process over. Turning off ad personalization by default, using a separate email for signups you don't fully trust, and periodically searching your own name are the habits that keep this from becoming a recurring project.

How to prioritize when you have a long list

If your list of accounts runs into the dozens, doing all of them at once isn't realistic and isn't necessary. A reasonable priority order: start with data brokers and people-search sites, since these are the ones visible to anyone who searches your name; then financial and healthcare-adjacent accounts, since these carry the most sensitive data; then social platforms; then everything else, roughly in order of how long it's been since you last used them. Treating this as a slow, ongoing project rather than a single weekend sprint is both more realistic and, since brokers repopulate periodically anyway, arguably more effective.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does the whole process take for one company?

A single request typically resolves within the legal deadline (30-45 days), though simple self-serve deletions can take effect within minutes to a few days.

Should I delete everything at once or one company at a time?

Either works, but tracking requests in a simple log becomes important once you're managing more than two or three at the same time.

Is there a tool that automates this whole checklist?

Paid monitoring services automate parts of it, particularly ongoing data broker rechecks — but every individual step here can be done manually at no cost.

What's the single most common mistake people make?

Sending a vague, unscoped request without citing a specific legal basis — it tends to get a slower and less complete response than a request that names the exact data and law involved.

How many companies should I expect to contact in a typical cleanup?

Most people who've never done this before find they have accounts and data traces across 15 to 30 companies once data brokers are included, though it varies widely based on how long you've been active online.

Is it worth hiring a service to do this instead?

It depends on your time versus your budget — every step in this checklist can be done for free manually, but a paid service saves the ongoing effort of rechecking data broker sites every few months.
